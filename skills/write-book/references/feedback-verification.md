# Outside feedback and certainty of findings

Read this when the author passes on an analysis from another model or a reviewer, when you review your own findings before applying fixes, and when an investigator, lawyer or protagonist in the text draws conclusions from circumstantial evidence.

## Treat outside feedback as a hypothesis

An analysis from another model or a reviewer, including your own from an earlier session, is a list of candidates to check, not an order. Before changing the text, for each item:

1. **Find the quoted fragment in the file.** If the "old fragment" does not exist, the item is a mistake (it happens: scenes and phrases are assigned to the wrong chapter). Reject it and record why.
2. **Read the context** (the paragraphs before and after). Check who speaks, to whom, and at what point of knowledge. A proposed fix may give a line to the wrong character, or kill a hypothesis that the scene already limits a few lines later.
3. **Grade it:**
   - an objective error (fact, date, statute, name, spelling): fix it;
   - a certainty error (a hypothesis given as fact): soften it where the narrator or text treats it as settled;
   - a style judgement: consider it, but do not apply it mechanically.

   Excessive hedging ("might have" in every line) ruins the rhythm of dialogue, so do not add it if the scene already marks the uncertainty.
4. **Check collisions** with later volumes and with phrases quoted verbatim.
5. **Record the decision** for each item: accepted, rejected (with reason), for the author, for a specialist.

Disagreement between reviewers is normal. When two independent analyses point at the same place, its weight grows. When one calls something an error and the other calls it correct, it is a matter for a specialist or the author, not for guessing. Admit your own earlier mistakes plainly and fix them (e.g. a missed statute, a wrong proposal).

## Certainty of findings in investigative and legal fiction

In crime, legal and financial fiction the character's conclusion runs ahead of the evidence. Keep three levels apart:

| Mark | Meaning | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | a fact shown in the text (document, scene, testimony) | a plain statement |
| **M** | the character's reconstruction (a hypothesis, an inference) | "he thought", "it could", "if they are right", "according to the contract"; the conclusion does not become fact in the next scene without evidence |
| **?** | what the text does not yet settle (a deliberate mystery) | leave it; do not settle it for the author |

Control questions for each important conclusion:
- What did the character see or receive in a document?
- What did they infer from it?
- Does the next scene treat it as a hypothesis or as a proven fact?

Typical slips:
- a similar format (e.g. code length) taken as proof of common origin;
- a receipt for "collecting money" taken as proof of payment;
- the absence of a transfer confirmation in the character's papers taken as proof that no transfer happened;
- the last known payment taken as years of funding;
- non-performance of a service stated when the text knows only that confirmation is missing;
- copy and original used in two meanings.

Do not weaken every statement. A character's certainty is a trait. The narrator's caveat should help the reader tell knowledge from belief. Mark a hypothesis as a hypothesis once and do not "harden" it later.

## Real-world detail: law, finance, technology

- Fiction does not excuse implausibility. Check the statute, the name of the authority, the register and the term at the source (the act, the official register, technical documentation), not from memory. Give the date and wording if the rule has changed (e.g. older limitation periods, historic cash-payment thresholds).
- Tell an obvious error (the wrong register, an authority that does not decide, a liability that does not pass to heirs) from a disputed or assumption-dependent matter (classification of an offence, transitional provisions). Fix the first. Mark the second "for a specialist to verify" in the report and in the volume description.
- When a legal fix breaks the construction of a scene (e.g. a debt that could not have passed to the heirs), do not patch one word.
  - Propose 2–3 variants of a changed premise, with the consequences for later volumes.
  - Choose the smallest one consistent with the notes and with the characters' text.
  - Leave the decision to the author, or record it as settled by their instruction.
- Working notes (names of mechanisms, your own legal shorthand) are not the book. Check whether a word appears in the prose at all before building a description on it.
- Check technical anachronisms separately (e.g. copying floppy disks with a command of the period is correct; whether deleted data survives depends on the file system).
